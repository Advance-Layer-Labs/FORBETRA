# Forbetra — Positioning & Go-to-Market Notes

_Companion to `canonical-model.md`. That doc defines **what** Forbetra is and how
it's built; this one is about **how we talk about it and sell it.** It keeps the
core belief intact and works through the marketability risks: vitamin-vs-painkiller,
education cost, the missing one-line hook, defensiveness, and reviewer fatigue._

Status: **draft / working notes.** Opinionated recommendations, not final.

---

## 0. The core message we are NOT changing

The belief stays exactly as in Marc's origin copy and `canonical-model.md §1`.

**Core message statement:**

> **You cannot become what you cannot see.**
>
> The goals that matter most — executive presence, competitiveness, confidence,
> leadership impact — can't be captured by a single metric. They live in how you
> show up, and in how others experience you. And how others experience you isn't
> separate from your effectiveness; it's part of it.
>
> Forbetra measures both sides. It tracks the **effort** you're making and the
> **performance** you're showing — as seen by you _and_ by the people around you —
> and how the **gap** between those views changes over time.
>
> That gap can expose a blind spot, reveal a hidden strength, or confirm a
> breakthrough already underway.
>
> Forbetra turns perception into progress you can **see, act on, and prove.**

**Tagline / dagger (for cold reach):**

> _You cannot become what you cannot see._

**Short form (one-liner):**

> _Forbetra turns how you see yourself and how others experience you into progress
> you can prove._

### The four principles behind Forbetra

1. **Important goals are not always directly measurable.**
   Goals like building executive presence, becoming more competitive, or
   increasing leadership impact are real, but they can't be captured by a single
   conventional metric.
2. **Development becomes trackable through what people do.**
   Change shows up in effort and performance. Both can be **rated and tracked**
   consistently — making an abstract goal concrete. (We track _perceptions_ of
   effort and performance, not objective measurements.)
3. **Effectiveness includes how others experience you.**
   Your own view matters, but so does how others perceive your effort and
   performance — and **the gap between the two views is where the real insight
   lives.**
4. **Progress is revealed over time.**
   When effort and performance are rated consistently — by you and by those around
   you — development becomes visible, actionable, and provable.

### The resulting idea

Forbetra turns an abstract goal into something you can track. It captures the
**effort** being made and the **performance** being demonstrated, and how both are
perceived by the **individual** and by **others** — including the **gap** between
those two views.

**Then it measures them again.**

That repetition, over a Journey, is how Forbetra shows whether development is
actually happening.

Everything below is about _packaging_ this so it lands faster and harder. The
philosophy ("streamlined by default, complexity hidden and triggered") applies to
marketing too: **lead with a sharp, concrete promise; reveal the deeper concept
only once they've leaned in.**

## 1. The single-sentence dagger

The origin copy is a great _story_ but has no one-line hook. Recommendation: don't
lead with the abstract concept ("measure the unmeasurable") — lead with a promise
tied to an audience. Keep the concept as the second beat.

**Master dagger (concept-level, for when audience is mixed):**

> **"Forbetra measures the goals you can't put a number on — and shows if you're
> actually getting better."**

**Audience-specific daggers (preferred for cold reach):**

- **Coaches / L&D:** _"Prove your coaching works."_ (backup: _"Turn 'I think
  you're improving' into proof."_)
- **Executives / ambitious individuals:** _"Know how you're really seen — before
  it's decided for you."_
- **Athletes / competitors:** _"See the gap between how hard you think you're going
  and how it actually looks."_

Rule of thumb: **the headline is the promise; the story is Marc's paragraph.** Never
open cold with the philosophy.

## 2. Vitamin → painkiller

The individual pitch is a vitamin (nice-to-have, low urgency). Painkillers attach
to a **painful, time-bound, high-stakes moment** where _not knowing your perception
has a real cost._ Strategies:

1. **Anchor to trigger moments,** not to "self-improvement in general":
   - Performance review / promotion cycle ("know before they decide").
   - Start of a coaching engagement ("baseline it, then prove the change").
   - New leadership role / first 90 days.
   - Athlete selection / pre-season / tryout window.
   - 360-review season (position as the lightweight, continuous alternative).
2. **Sell the painkiller to the buyer who feels the pain most: the coach / org.**
   For an individual, blind spots are uncomfortable; for a coach or an L&D budget
   owner, _unprovable ROI is an existential threat._ The ROI/accountability angle
   (canonical §1) is the painkiller — it converts "nice to know" into "I need this
   to justify the spend / keep the client / defend the budget."
3. **Make the cost of the gap concrete.** Reframe from "track your growth" to
   "the gap you can't see is the one that stalls your career / costs you the
   role / loses the game." Loss-aversion creates urgency that aspiration doesn't.
4. **Time-box the value.** A 12-week Journey with a baseline and an end report is
   inherently a "get a result by X" promise — lean into that as the painkiller
   deliverable ("in 12 weeks you'll have proof, not a hunch").

## 3. Keeping the education cost low

"Nebulous-goal measurement" is an unnamed category, which is expensive to teach.
Lower the cost by **showing, not defining:**

1. **Lead with the artifact, not the theory.** The demo-able hook is the
   **gap visual** — two lines (you vs. them) drifting apart or converging over a
   Journey. One picture teaches the whole concept faster than any paragraph.
2. **Borrow an existing mental shelf.** People already understand "360 feedback,"
   "fitness tracker for progress," and "blind spot." Position as: _"a continuous,
   lightweight 360 — for the goals a 360 can't score"_ or _"a progress tracker for
   the things you can't step on a scale to measure."_ Borrowed categories cost far
   less to teach than net-new ones.
3. **One concrete example beats a definition.** Always pair the pitch with a
   worked case ("'Be more decisive' → your reviewers watch for it → week 1 you
   score yourself 8, they score you 5 → by week 10 the gap is 1"). Concreteness
   removes the "wait, how does this even work?" moment.
4. **Progressive disclosure in the product _is_ the education.** Onboarding asks
   for the minimum; the concept reveals itself as data accumulates. Don't teach
   the philosophy up front — let the first gap they see teach it.

## 4. Getting around defensiveness

"Find out what others really think of you" is exciting to some, threatening to
others — and **reveal-on (canonical decision #5) raises the stakes**, so framing
matters more, not less.

1. **Frame the gap as a gift, not a grade.** Language: "blind spot," "discovery,"
   "the view you can't get on your own" — never "score," "judgment," "rating of
   you." The AI insight narrative should be constructive and curious, never
   verdict-like.
2. **Give the individual agency and go first.** They choose their reviewers, they
   set the goal, and they self-rate + set the baseline _before_ anyone else weighs
   in. Starting from their own view (agency) softens the arrival of others' views.
3. **Lead with Effort (self-controllable) alongside Performance.** Effort is
   within the person's control, so it's less threatening; pairing it with
   performance keeps the experience from feeling like pure external judgment.
4. **Normalize the gap.** "Almost everyone has a 1–3 point gap — that's the point,
   not a failing." Set the expectation that a gap is _the normal, useful signal_,
   so seeing one feels like insight rather than criticism.
5. **Celebrate convergence and improvement, not just deltas.** Make "the gap
   closed" and "you improved from baseline" the emotional payoff — progress framing
   over judgment framing.
6. **Position reveal as _enabling the conversation_, not exposure.** Marc's intent
   for reveal-on is organic individual↔reviewer conversations. Sell it that way:
   "so you can actually talk about it," not "so everyone sees your scores."

## 5. Reviewer experience & fatigue

This is the make-or-break: the value depends on reviewers _sustaining_ a behavior
we can't market away. Below is what the product already does well, then the gaps.

**Already good (keep / protect):**

- **No account, ~60 seconds, two questions + optional note** — token-link feedback,
  extremely low friction. This is the single most important thing; protect it
  fiercely.
- **Auto-throttle** caps pending requests so a reviewer is never flooded.
- **Weekly cadence** with a **less-frequent option** for low-engagement reviewers
  (canonical decision #4) — meet them where they are.
- **Welcome + reminder + impact-summary** touchpoints already exist.

**Where we can make it genuinely enjoyable (recommendations):**

1. **Show impact immediately, not just monthly.** The moment a reviewer submits,
   show them something ("your input moved the picture" / a glimpse of alignment) —
   with reveal-on we can show how their view compared. Immediate feedback loops are
   what make participation feel worthwhile.
2. **Make the ask feel meaningful and personal.** "Alex specifically chose you
   because you see them in X" beats a generic "please rate." People help people,
   not forms.
3. **Keep the required surface tiny; make richer input optional.** Effort +
   Performance are the required two taps; `behavioralObservation` and `suggestion`
   should be **progressive/optional**, never gating the 60-second promise.
4. **Reciprocity & recognition.** Thank meaningfully; consider showing reviewers
   the difference they made over a Journey ("you helped Alex close a 4-point gap").
   The stakeholder-impact summary is the seed of this — make it feel like a
   highlight, not a report.
5. **Respect attention as the scarce resource.** Never more than weekly; smart
   skip if they just responded; let them dial frequency down without guilt. A
   reviewer who isn't nagged stays a reviewer.
6. **Make it mobile-instant.** The link should open to a two-tap rating with zero
   loading friction — the difference between a 90% and a 40% completion rate.

**The reframe:** treat reviewers as a **second user type we design _for_,** not a
data source we extract from. Their delight is a growth engine (they may become
individuals themselves), and their fatigue is the #1 churn risk for the whole loop.
